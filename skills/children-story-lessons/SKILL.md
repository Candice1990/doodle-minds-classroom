# Story-led CS lessons

## Established classroom context
For this user's Doodle Minds lessons, unless changed: ages 5–7, 12 children, six partner pairs, 60 minutes; projected English interface operated by the teacher. Explain work to the user in Chinese. Screen copy speaks to children; classroom logistics stay in an existing teacher-only area when useful.

Do not generate a separate lesson plan, Chinese teaching-plan file, or companion teaching document unless the user explicitly requests one. Default deliverables are the interactive lesson and needed printable child materials. Do not remove existing guides merely because this default changed.

## Content and difficulty
- Read the course framework or standards supplied in the project before designing or substantially revising a lesson. Use its entry-level progression; do not treat later-stage targets as beginner requirements.
- Before choosing a story or game, name the CS concept and the observable behavior that will demonstrate understanding. Success should require predicting, arranging, executing, testing or explaining computer behavior; recognizing English words alone must not solve the lesson.
- Teach **one core concept per lesson through 4–5 concrete examples, with at least four** for this user's courses. Change the situation, not the knowledge point. Repeat the same child-friendly question and interaction pattern across examples.
- Make **each lesson’s own core concept and keyword a recurring thread across the whole lesson**. Use the actual topic for that lesson (for example, `sequence` in Lesson 1); do not carry the previous lesson’s keyword into a new topic by default. Reinforce it as often as naturally useful in the introduction, mission/question, activity labels, prediction, execution, retry/success feedback, voice, paper creation, sharing and closing. Tie every repetition to what children are doing or observing so it builds understanding, not just word recognition.
- Keep reinforcement concise and consistent: one simple definition, a short recurring question or refrain, and invitations for children to point, say, act, test and explain the concept. Prefer replacing existing labels and prompts over adding text, badges or panels. Maintain clear structure and logical progression; do not crowd the screen to increase keyword frequency or obscure a concrete error explanation.
- Avoid contrasts between unfamiliar or near-synonymous terms that may confuse children. For this user, do not use “Same steps. New sequence.” or similar steps-versus-sequence slogans. Prefer a direct action-and-prediction prompt such as “We changed our sequence. What happens now?” Adapt the phrasing to each lesson’s actual concept.
- Keep examples simple enough to act out and match the confirmed concept. The approved Pumpkin Party Lesson 1 teaches **sequence: the computer executes instructions in order; changing the order can change the result**. Do not revert it to word recognition or a vague-instruction lesson. A different lesson specifically about clear instructions can use individual actions without adding sequencing, conditions or navigation.
- Repeat the concept and interaction while gradually reducing help: observe a mixed-up program and change it → build a program → transfer to a new situation → investigate a useful variation → plan and explain independently. This is a progression example, not a mandatory template for every concept.
- Meet the character before introducing the mission. Establish their goal, what they have and the obstacle before asking a question. Show the whole adventure before individual challenges.
- State the interaction: choose or arrange something → character executes it → visible result. A prediction must not look like an extra condition.
- Explain physical constraints and machine capabilities before testing; let children discover the effect of their program through prediction, execution and observation. Do not introduce a new restriction after a failed attempt. Keep correct command order out of introductory copy unless intentionally demonstrating it.
- Use YES/NO for a condition's observed answer; IF/ELSE when deliberately explaining the program. Prefer concrete actions or objects (boat, balloon) over abstract destinations (river, sky).
- Align every card, symbol, name and result across screen, animation and paper. Include matching/nonmatching cases and useful variations or boundaries when relevant to the concept.
- Real-life sequences may have several valid orders. If a demonstration prescribes an order, establish the plan; distinguish following it from physical feasibility. For vague instructions, explain that people can sometimes infer intent from context; Pip's hesitation is a teaching model.

## Page progression and mission cards
- Give every page a clear purpose and bridge to the next. For the approved lesson, use greeting → whole-party overview → individual mission card → prediction/program building → execution/observation → retry → next mission → partner making/testing → sharing. Keep advancement teacher-operated.
- Before each example's activity appears, show a separate mission card with a large goal picture, brief motivation, starting props/state, concrete machine rules and a question such as “How can we reach this goal?” A teacher click opens the activity. The card explains what counts as the goal and relevant failure conditions without presenting a numbered solution.
- Move long setup prose into the mission card. Keep the activity focused on the goal, program, action and result. Provide a way to revisit the mission without discarding the chosen cards.
- Make machine constraints understandable and physically coherent. An open card receives a star inside; a folded card receives it outside. Explain invented machine constraints, such as a cover blocking a loading slot, before use. Accept every order that satisfies the stated model; do not manufacture a unique answer by rejecting reasonable alternatives.

## Execution, errors and goal evidence
- Run the submitted instructions against actual state, including unsuccessful actions. Do not automatically reorder, supply missing steps or repair an earlier mistake. Later actions may change state only when they really can; retain consequences that remain, such as spilled sweets or a stamp on the empty table.
- Separate completing an action from reaching the goal. A posted card can be blank inside; a smiling pumpkin can still be on the work table; a balloon can be tied and on the hook but remain flat. Judge all stated goal conditions, not just the last action or a predetermined answer string.
- At the failed step, hold the visible consequence, mark the relevant object/location and instruction, show Pip's puzzled expression and play a gentle error cue. Use a symbol as well as color. Continue the remaining program after observation; retain Stop and retry.
- At the end, show the actual executed result and specific unmet conditions beside the goal. Derive the result drawing from state rather than reusing the desired goal illustration. A generic “Oops” or “wrong order” alone is insufficient. Inspect hidden properties visibly, for example open a posted invitation to show its real inside.
- Keep the failed-step explanation and final-result explanation distinct: the former says what happened then; the latter describes what is missing now. Preserve valid alternatives: the approved lantern accepts light → cover → on and light → on → cover.

## Standalone themed series
For Pumpkin Party, each lesson must be independently accessible: no prerequisite lesson, previous artifact or stored gallery record. Reintroduce Pip and today's mission; provide all needed vocabulary, cards and materials. A common world is continuity, not a dependency. Each example supplies its own starting props and state. ## Character greeting and existing assets
- Before the main activity, show Pip on his own. Invite the children to wave and say “Hello, Pip!” together. A teacher click then makes Pip wave, show “Hello!” and play the existing, single-word Hello recording. Advance after the reply; allow replay.
- Find the original Doodle Minds logo and Pip greeting in the `CS for kids` project instead of substituting a text brand, generic icon or newly synthesized greeting. Known references are `demo-class/assets/doodle-minds-logo.png` and `demo-class/assets/pip-hello-recorded.wav` (also present in `Halloween-Topic`). Inspect and verify the actual files.
- Reuse through explicit paths or copy approved assets into the target lesson's assets folder for portability. Preserve the original greeting; do not assume authorization to clone a personal voice.

## Approved visual and animation direction
Keep warm cream/sage/coral, original SVG illustration, large projected text and playful picture cards. Build a richly illustrated world rather than a sparse character on an empty background. For Pumpkin Party, use friendly Halloween elements: smiling jack-o'-lanterns, hanging pumpkin lanterns, moon and stars, bats, candy, a small party house, cobwebs and a cute spider. Halloween is playful, not frightening.

Keep instructional pictures simple: one dominant object/action per card, consistent with the scene and printable symbols. Avoid miniature multi-object compositions, repeated banks of already-selected cards and permanently visible editing controls that obscure the program. Show unused choices while building; give the ordered program visual priority.

Layer atmosphere behind the teaching objects. Keep the actor, candidate objects, instruction and outcome visually distinct. Decorations and signs must not obscure object labels, motion paths or thought bubbles. Subtle lantern sway, bat drift and glowing lights can enliven waiting states; pause decorative motion during execution so the instructional action dominates.

Animate the cause, not just the outcome: highlight the current card → start the action → reach/contact the target → reveal the change → hold for observation. Ink appears when the stamp touches, and the stamp sound plays at contact; do not reveal the final state before its cause. For SVG, separate fixed placement from animated transforms so motion does not overwrite the object’s origin.

Give actions a visible starting position, movement and held result: the hat travels to Pip's head, the pumpkin moves from table to hand, the letter goes to the named recipient. Teacher-paced progression remains essential; visual richness must not add knowledge points or automatic chapter advancement.

## Classroom layout
- The desktop classroom lesson must fit one viewport without vertical page scrolling. Use available viewport height for the header, chapters, heading, main panels and bottom navigation; keep both main panels equal in height and their tops/bottoms aligned. Let the scene and program area adapt to the remaining space.
- Preserve readable pictures, text and click targets by reducing repeated content, excess gaps and padding before shrinking everything. Do not hide overflow or crop essential controls to claim the page fits. Success/failure feedback and the partner-task selector must fit too; a contained teacher guide or long printable sheet can scroll separately.
- Verify common classroom and window sizes, including 1024×600, 1366×768 and a larger desktop viewport. Check actual page and panel overflow, button bounds and visual balance. Keep narrow layouts usable rather than forcing an unreadably scaled desktop screen.

## Emotional feedback and sound
- **Every actual error and unmet goal needs a short spoken explanation of its specific cause**, in addition to visible feedback. A generic “Oops” or “try again” alone does not satisfy this requirement. Use one or two clear child-friendly sentences, usually about 3–6 seconds: name the relevant object/action and what happened or why it could not work. Derive the explanation from the executed state; distinguish a failed action, a missing instruction and an unmet final goal. Retain the lesson’s keyword where it helps, without stretching the explanation or claiming that a valid alternative is wrong. When muted or playback fails, keep the same explanation visible and allow the lesson to continue.
- Keep errors gentle: Pip looks puzzled, marks the relevant card/object, and gives a brief descending cue with the specific explanation. Make retry easy; do not shame the child or exaggerate distress. Reuse an explanation across genuinely identical causes, but give different causes distinct recordings. Do not mark voice work complete until the relevant local recordings are generated, mapped and verified.
- Success gets a happy expression (smiling eyes and cheeks), a small celebratory hop, stars/confetti, an outcome badge and varied encouragement such as “Yeah! You got it!”, “Great job!” or “Congratulations!” Match the spoken phrase, visible praise and actual task result.
- Use natural, expressive stock/neural voices for feedback. The basic macOS Samantha `say` clips were rejected as mechanical. For this user’s English classroom feedback and error explanations, prefer the approved American English female child voice `en-US-AnaNeural`, at near-default pitch and rate. Preserve the recorded Hello. If Ana is unavailable, report the blocker before substituting another voice; do not pitch-shift basic speech to imitate a child.
- When generating or revising voice assets, read [Ana voice generation and playback](references/ana-voice-generation.md) for the verified local workflow, October 2026 Edge endpoint compatibility fix, and audio QA.
- Make short local audio assets so the classroom lesson runs offline. Provide a compact combined listening sample when changing voice quality. Distinguish synthesized feedback from the original recorded Hello and from lesson narration.
- Keep voice intelligible by lowering overlapping effects. Use distinct action cues and a short celebration melody/applause without drowning out speech. Wait for playback completion, with an error/timeout fallback, before unlocking edits/navigation; do not cut off a longer replacement clip using the old fixed delay.
- Sound defaults on and starts after a user gesture. Include a toggle. Stop/mute/navigation cancels all active voice and effect sources and pending callbacks. Replay starts audio from the beginning without overlap. Visual feedback still works when muted or audio fails.

## Child participation and artifacts
- For this user, printable worksheets are individual activities: omit partner/group work, role swapping and “tell others”/sharing prompts. Keep the paper task focused on two selected example sets rather than asking children to complete every on-screen example; provide clear, generous spaces for arranging the matching picture cards and checking the result.
- Child-facing worksheets contain only directions children need for the activity. Do not include teacher prompts, answer guidance or classroom logistics in the printable PDFs.
- **Deliver all child worksheets as ready-to-print PDF files.** HTML may remain as an editable source, but an HTML print page or instructions to “Save as PDF” are not the finished worksheet deliverable. Export the actual worksheets to stable, clearly named PDF files and make the lesson’s worksheet/print links open those PDFs directly. Use A4 landscape at 100% unless the material or user calls for another format; keep browser headers/footers off, proper margins, matching picture symbols and precut-friendly cards. Render and visually inspect every final PDF page for clipping, overlap, missing graphics/fonts and usable writing/cutting space. Verify page size and page count before delivery, and regenerate the PDFs whenever their source worksheets change. Keep PDFs with the project and provide direct file links to the user.

Plan participation, not padded teacher talk. For 60 minutes use approximately 5–8 minutes introduction, 20–30 shared exploration, 17–22 partner making/testing and 7–10 sharing/closing; adapt without exceeding the total. Precut parts when appropriate. Every child's artifact must be runnable, testable or explainable. Decoration follows the core task. Supply a scaffolded option and a real extension that practices the same concept, rather than lengthening the program by default.

## Implementation and verification
- Execute a frozen submitted program. Actor, highlighted instruction, trace and result agree. Disable edits/navigation during execution; retain safe Stop, audio mute and recovery. Rerun starts cleanly.
- Pause before execution and hold each instructional state for observation. Offer slower pacing. For repetition lessons, distinguish body, round number and total actions.
- Respect reduced motion while retaining instructional states and expressions. Support keyboard and focus; do not depend solely on dragging or color. Keep alternate SVG facial expressions hidden by default so printable icons do not display several expressions at once.
- Preserve completed Wonder Garden files and unrelated lessons. Build one requested lesson at a time in its own directory; make explicitly requested revisions there.
- Verify logic and teaching expression separately: a failed predicate does not prove children can see the failure. For small programs, enumerate all permutations and confirm actual success sets, failed-step detection, final goal conditions and state-driven drawings; retain valid alternate orders. Then inspect representative correct and incorrect runs in the browser, including the intermediate action and held final result. Verify incorrect/empty inputs, animation lifecycle, stop/replay/reset, navigation, mission-card entry/review, audio toggle and asset availability. Test both success and failure voices, actual media duration/playback errors and cancellation. File existence or a successful TTS command does not prove a nonempty recording. Do not claim subjective listening quality from waveform or playback-state checks alone.
- Use supported browser tools for visual QA. Check welcome, mission cards, all activity chapters and partner making, with empty/partial/full programs and running/success/failure states. Confirm no classroom-page scrolling, equal panel heights, readable action/result and visible navigation at the classroom sizes above; also inspect a narrow layout. Scope scene-SVG sizing to the scene root so card icons are not enlarged accidentally. Verify printed materials for clipping and overlapping labels.
- Refresh after edits and version updated media/script references when necessary to avoid stale cached feedback. Disclose unverified audio playback. Where saving creations is requested, offer local save/export/import, validate imported data and render names as text.
